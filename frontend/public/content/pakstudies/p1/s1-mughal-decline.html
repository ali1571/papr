REASONS FOR THE DECLINE OF MUGHAL EMPIRE

INTERNAL CAUSES

Aurangzebs policies:

  • His intolerance towards non-Muslims, imposition of Jizya (tax on non-Muslims), destruction of Hindu temples, banning of suttee and suppression of Hindu practices alienated the majority Hindu population.
  • Aurangzeb imposed higher taxes (5% custom duty) on non-Muslims traders compared to Muslims traders (2%), creating resentment among Hindus and other religious groups. His favourtism towards Sunni Muslims and persecution of Shias and other religious groups weakened internal unity and support for the empire.
  • Non-Muslims were excluded from high-ranking positions, limiting their economic and social progress, which further alienated them.

Economic Decline:

  • Aurangzeb’s extravagant spending on wars, palaces, and mosques drained the treasury.
  • Heavy taxation to fund military campaigns, like the Deccan wars (against marathas), caused widespread discontent among the people.
  • The empire’s economy weakened, leading to financial instability and contributing to its downfall.

Weak and Incompetent Successors:

  • After Aurangzeb, his successors were lazy, corrupt, and incompetent. They focused on luxury and pleasure rather than governance.
  • they spent time gambling, music and drinking. they wore fine clothes and jewellery. they became complacent and thought that success will go on forever.

Extravagant Life:

  • fine buildings were symbol of power and culture. Mughal spent ridiculously on Fine masjids and palaces. e.g Aurangzeb built a personal Pearl Masjid for his own prayers.
  • there used to be hundred courses of meal at royal courts. after aurangzeb his incompetent successor became more extravagant, leading to financial issues.

absence of a clear line of succession / infighting :

  • Led to infighting and wars of succession, weakening the empire’s stability.
  • these wars got worse after aurangzebs death, 12 different ppl tried to claim the mughal throne in 10 years. mughal palaces became places of confusion and chaos.
  • this weakened main administration as there was no strong leader at the centre. this made Every mughal enemy confident.

Military inefficiency:

  • as mughals became complacent they didnt put effort in upgrading their army. as a result the power of army declined. it didnt remain as strong. mughals let their army expertise declined, to extent that they couldnt defeat marathas.
  • this led to decline of mughal empire.

Rise of Regional Powers:

  • The Marathas, Sikhs, and other regional groups gained strength and challenged Mughal authority. Aurangzeb spent 25yrs trying to subdue Marathas, this showed how weak mughal army had become.
  • looking at the centre of mughals decline provincial governors (nawabs) and local princes rebelled, declaring independence and refusing to pay taxes to the central government.

Administrative Challenges:

  • The vastness of the empire made it difficult to administer and defend effectively. it took days for a message to reach Delhi from a corner of the empire. pathans in the nwfp, marathas in deccan and sikhs under ranjit singh in punjab, posed great threat to the sprawling but weak mughal empire.
  • Corruption among nobles and the decline of the Mansabdari system further weakened the administration.

EXTERNAL CAUSES

Nadir Shah’s Invasion (1739):

  • The Persian ruler Nadir Shah invaded the Mughal Empire, sacked Delhi, and looted its wealth, including the Peacock Throne and precious jewels.
  • His invasion exposed the weakness of the Mughal Empire, shattering its prestige and revealing its vulnerability to foreign powers.

Ahmad Shah Abdali’s Invasions (1748–1767):

  • The Afghan ruler Ahmad Shah Abdali invaded India multiple times, defeating the Marathas at the Third Battle of Panipat (1761).
  • Although he captured territories like Kabul, Peshawar, Lahore, and Kashmir, he failed to consolidate his rule and returned to Afghanistan.
  • His invasions further weakened the Mughal Empire and encouraged regional powers like the Marathas and Sikhs to challenge Mughal authority.

British expansion:

  • The British, empowered by the Industrial Revolution, had superior military technology, economic resources, and well-trained armies.
  • They used the Mughal Empire’s weakness, using modern weapons and strategic tactics to rapidly expand their control over India.
  • The British East India Company’s confident and determined forces easily defeated the poorly trained and weakened Mughal armies, leading to the empire’s eventual collapse.

why were marathas defeated after the mughal decline [7]

  • Defeat at the Battle of Panipat (1761): The Marathas suffered a devastating defeat at the Third Battle of Panipat against Ahmad Shah Abdali (Durrani) of Afghanistan. The Maratha army, though large, was poorly organized and lacked coordination, while Abdali’s forces were tactically superior. The loss of key leaders, including the Peshwa’s son, and the death of thousands of soldiers crippled Maratha power and halted their expansion.
  • Internal Disunity and Weak Leadership: After the decline of the Mughals, the Maratha Confederacy became fragmented, with regional leaders (like Sindhia, Holkar, and Bhonsle) prioritizing their own interests over collective goals. The lack of a strong central authority and infighting among Maratha chiefs weakened their ability to resist external threats and maintain control over their territories.
  • Rise of the British: The British East India Company, with its superior military technology, disciplined army, and strategic diplomacy, exploited the Marathas’ disunity. The Marathas were defeated in a series of Anglo-Maratha Wars (1775–1818), as they could not match the British’s organized forces and modern warfare tactics. The British also formed alliances with rival Indian powers, further isolating and weakening the Marathas.

Who were Zamindars?

  • Zamindars were landlords and tax collectors during the Mughal Empire. They belonged to the nobility and were wealthy and influential.
  • Some Zamindars had private armies and controlled large areas of land. They played a key role in local administration and revenue collection.
  • After Aurangzeb’s death, Zamindars became increasingly unwilling to accept the authority of new emperors. They refused to pay taxes or revenues to the central government in Delhi, weakening the empire’s financial stability.
  • Zamindars often rebelled against Mughal rule, further destabilizing the empire.
