  • European nations like the Portuguese and Dutch had already established trading bases in India.

Large Population and Market Potential:

  • India’s large population offered a vast market for British manufactured goods.
  • The EIC aimed to exploit this market to sell their products and secure cheap raw materials for their industries.

BATTLE OF PLASSEY 1757

The battle took place on the banks of the Bhagirathi River, near Calcutta, between Siraj-ud-Daulah, the Nawab of Bengal, and the British East India Company (EIC).

Siraj-ud-Daulah had become Nawab in 1756 and opposed the EIC’s fortification activities.

Encouraged by the French, Siraj-ud-Daulah attacked and captured Calcutta in 1756, but the British retaliated under Robert Clive.

Robert Clive bribed Mir Jafar, Siraj-ud-Daulah’s commander, to betray the Nawab during the battle.

Despite having a larger army of 50,000 soldiers, 40 cannons, and 10 war elephants, Siraj-ud-Daulah’s forces were defeated by Clive’s 3,000 troops.

Siraj-ud-Daulah was killed after the battle, and his body was found in a river.

The EIC gained control over Bengal’s trade and revenue, marking the beginning of British dominance in India.

Robert Clive was appointed Governor of Bengal, and Mir Jafar was installed as a puppet Nawab.

The battle consolidated British power in Bengal, which later expanded across India over the next century.

ANGLO MYSORE WARS 1767-1799

The Anglo-Mysore Wars were a series of four conflicts fought between the Kingdom of Mysore (led by Hyder Ali and later his son Tipu Sultan) and the British East India Company (EIC) in the late 18th century.

First Anglo-Mysore War (1767–1769):

Hyder Ali, the ruler of Mysore, successfully resisted British forces and almost captured Madras. The war ended with the Treaty of Madras, which restored the status quo but left tensions unresolved.

Second Anglo-Mysore War (1780–1784):

Haider Ali formed an alliance with the French and fought against the British, Marathas, and the Nizam of Hyderabad. The war saw fluctuating fortunes, with Hyder Ali achieving early successes but ultimately dying in 1782. His son, Tipu Sultan, continued the fight, and the war ended with the Treaty of Mangalore, which maintained the pre-war boundaries.

Third Anglo-Mysore War (1789–1792):

Tipu Sultan attacked Travancore, a British ally, leading to the Third Anglo-Mysore War. The British, Marathas, and the Nizam of Hyderabad formed a coalition against Mysore. The war ended with the Treaty of Seringapatam (1792), forcing Tipu Sultan to cede half of his kingdom and pay a large indemnity to the British.

Fourth Anglo-Mysore War (1799):

The final war saw the British, supported by the Marathas and the Nizam of Hyderabad, launch a coordinated attack on Mysore from all sides. Tipu Sultan’s forces were outnumbered, with 35,000 troops against the British-led coalition’s 60,000. The British achieved a decisive victory at the Siege of Seringapatam, where Tipu Sultan was killed defending his capital. Mysore was dismantled, and the British installed a puppet ruler, effectively ending Mysore’s independence.

WHAT WAS THE BLACK HOLE TRAGEDY?

  • In 1756, Siraj-ud-Daulah, the Nawab of Bengal, attacked and captured Fort William in Calcutta, defeating the British forces.
  • The British residents and soldiers were taken as prisoners.
  • Approximately 146 British prisoners were confined in a small, poorly ventilated cell known as the "Black Hole" of Fort William.
  • Due to extreme heat, overcrowding, and lack of ventilation, 123 out of 146 prisoners died overnight from suffocation and heat exhaustion.
  • Only 23 survivors were found the next morning.
